Case Study · Ionex Energy

iEnergy 2026

The next-generation management console for EV battery-swap networks — bringing dashboard, assets, swap operations, customer care, and billing into one coherent place.

Role
Lead Product Designer — research, IA, interaction & visual design
Timeline
2025 → 2026
Scope
Dashboard · Assets · Swap Ops · Customer Care · Billing
Platform
Web console, built on the IonexOS design system
01 — Overview

From a monitoring console to an operating system for energy networks

The first iEnergy Console (2019–2022) proved that operators could monitor a battery-swap network from a single screen. By 2025, the network had outgrown it: hundreds of stations, thousands of batteries, and new business lines — fleet rental, customer service, billing — each running in its own tool.

iEnergy 2026 is a ground-up redesign. Instead of one dashboard with everything bolted on, it is structured as five focused modules that share one data model, one navigation pattern, and one design language.

02 — The Problem

Complex data, many stakeholders, zero patience for downtime

Every minute a station is down, riders can't swap batteries. The people keeping the network alive range from field technicians to executives — and the legacy tools forced all of them through the same generic views.

a.

Multi-level user hierarchy

Admins, staff, and operators need different access levels and different views of the same underlying data — one size fits nobody.

b.

Real-time critical alerts

Swap failures and station outages must be visible and actionable within seconds, not buried in an event log.

c.

Fragmented workflows

A single customer complaint could touch swap records, battery health, and billing — three separate tools, three separate logins.

d.

Revenue & analytics

Leadership needed swap patterns, revenue trends, and utilisation insights for planning — previously assembled by hand in spreadsheets.

03 — Research

Shadowing the people who keep the network running

I ran contextual inquiries with station operators, customer-service agents, and management — shadowing real shifts in operation centers rather than interviewing in a meeting room. Three findings shaped the entire architecture:

i.

Roles read different dashboards

Technicians live in battery-level diagnostics; executives live in KPIs and trends. The homepage must adapt to the role, not average across them.

ii.

History is how problems get solved

Most troubleshooting time is spent reconstructing what happened. Complete, filterable event and swap histories are not a nice-to-have.

iii.

Cases cross module boundaries

A complaint starts in customer care but resolves in swap records or billing. The system must let one case travel across modules without losing context.

"I'd rather see everything on one screen and squint, than click five times to find out why a swap failed."
Station operator, field study
04 — The Solution

Five modules, one mental model

Each module owns one job and hands off cleanly to the next. Shared patterns — tables, filters, detail drawers, status colors — mean learning one module teaches you all five.

1.

Dashboard

Role-based overview with live KPIs, network map, and a prioritized alert feed — critical events surface first, always.

2.

Asset Management

Every station, cabinet, and battery with its health, lifecycle, and maintenance history — from network level down to a single cell.

3.

Swap Operations

Complete swap records with failure diagnostics, filterable by station, rider, battery, or error type — built for root-cause hunting.

4.

Customer Care

Complaint cases linked directly to the rider's swap history and battery data, so agents resolve issues without leaving the case.

5.

Billing

Plans, invoices, and adjustments with a full audit trail — transparent for finance, traceable for customer care.

+

Shared foundations

One permission model, one search, one notification center. The modules feel like rooms in the same building, not separate buildings.

05 — Interface

A command center that stays calm under pressure

The console uses IonexOS dark-mode tokens: a deep navy surface that reduces eye strain over long shifts, with the brand blue reserved for live data and a strict three-tier alert color system. Density is embraced, but hierarchy makes it scannable.

06 — System

Built on IonexOS, and feeding back into it

iEnergy 2026 was the first product designed entirely on the IonexOS design system — every table, drawer, and status indicator comes from the shared component library. In return, the console's data-dense patterns (DataTable, StatusIndicator, OrganizationPicker) were contributed back to the system for every other Ionex product to use.

08 — Learnings

What this project taught me

i.

Density is a feature in B2B

Operators prefer information density with strong hierarchy over simplicity that hides critical data behind clicks.

ii.

Test in real conditions

Operation-center testing revealed what the studio never could — screen glare, time pressure, and the need for larger targets.

iii.

Systems compound

Designing on IonexOS meant new modules shipped in weeks, not months — and every pattern we invented made the next product faster.
